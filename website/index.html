No Pinhole Proxies

Measurement-consistent Gaussian splatting for fisheye–LiDAR rigs

NIZAHO · Chuan project

Read the story ↓Validation planExplore scenesCode & draft ↗

TL;DR

Gaussian splatting from fisheye–LiDAR rigs looks right but measures wrong. We trace this to supervision: each sensor is replaced by a pinhole proxy, and the proxies disagree about what “depth” is, so geometry yields to appearance. We instead render every fisheye pixel and every LiDAR beam as the same ray with one termination depth, let the LiDAR evidence along those rays decide which degrees of freedom the photometric loss may move, and keep every acceleration of the existing CUDA trainer. All three are hypotheses with explicit kill criteria; none has been measured yet.

01The gap

Photorealistic, but not metric

Handheld scanners, backpack and vehicle mapping systems and 360° capture rigs all pair wide-angle cameras with a LiDAR. Their users want one model that both looks right and measures right: a digital twin you can walk through and also put a tape measure on.

Gaussian splatting [1] delivered the first half. The second half has lagged. On Oxford Spires [2], whose terrestrial laser scans (TLS) give independent ground truth at 1 cm, the official LiDAR maps reach an F-score at 10 cm of 0.54–0.85 across sequences, while a radiance field trained on the same captures reaches 0.31–0.68.

The camera side has since been repaired. Native fisheye renderers [3] [4] [5] [6] stopped cutting the fisheye into perspective crops, which discarded field of view and resolution. That makes the next question unavoidable: does the geometric gap close once the camera is native and the LiDAR is used as depth supervision?

Key takeaway

We do not know yet, and the plan is built around that. Experiment 1 measures it before anything else. If native-fisheye splatting with LiDAR supervision already matches the LiDAR scans it was trained on, there is no gap to explain, and the rest of this page is withdrawn.

02Diagnosis

Inverse rendering is not inverse measurement

Robotic mapping treats every reading as a measurement with a forward sensor model, from occupancy grids [10] to TSDF fusion [11]. Radiance fields treat images as rendering targets [12] [1]. With a single camera the two views coincide, because a rendered pixel is the measurement. A LiDAR breaks the symmetry.

The inverse-rendering answer is to manufacture a second rendering proxy so that laser returns become “pixels”. Our own baseline R1, the working system of §07, is a representative example. It replaces each fisheye image with perspective crops rasterised through the affine EWA projection [8], whose error grows off-axis [9], and each LiDAR sweep with a virtual pinhole depth camera at the LiDAR origin (120° field of view, 256 × 256 queries) that renders

D_{\mathrm{R1}}(q) \;=\; \sum_i \; z_i\;\alpha_i\,\mathcal G_i(q)\;T_i(q)

Read it from the inside out. z_i is the camera-axis depth of the centre of Gaussian i, \alpha_i\mathcal G_i(q) is how much of its projected 2D footprint covers query q, and T_i(q) is the light that survives the Gaussians in front. The sum is an opacity-weighted average of centre depths, compared with the depth of the measured return through 0.1\log(1+10\,|D_{\mathrm{R1}}-z_{\mathrm{meas}}|).

Each row is a different definition of depth applied to the same Gaussians, and they fail in three distinct ways.

i · Quantity

Centre, not range

z_i is constant across the footprint, so a 50 cm Gaussian lying obliquely on a wall reports its centre depth to every beam that crosses its edge. The LiDAR measures range along its own beam.

ii · Statistic

Mean, median, first hit

An expectation, a median and a first surface coexist. A faint floater in front of a wall drags the expectation into empty space long before the median notices.

iii · Gradient

No push along the beam

Neither depth term differentiates z_i. Geometry can change only by fading Gaussians or sliding footprints sideways, never by moving a Gaussian along the line of sight.

What to watch

  1. Raise the floater’s opacity. The baseline’s expected depth slides into empty space while the measured return stays put.
  2. Tilt the wall. The beam now meets the Gaussian away from its centre, and the proxy keeps reporting the centre depth.
  3. Push the floater past one half. Every median, including ours, jumps to the floater. In the ray model that is exactly what the free-space term and the “contradicted” state are there to catch.

Key takeaway

The shared Gaussians receive conflicting targets, and the cheapest way for the optimiser to reconcile them is to let appearance absorb the conflict. That is our explanation of the gap. It is falsifiable: if replacing the proxies does not move the result (Experiment 2), the explanation is wrong.

Share the Gaussians, or split them?

Sensor-simulation work already renders both sensors natively, but keeps them apart. SplatAD [13] gives the LiDAR its own spherical rasteriser and renders expected range in training but median range at inference; Uni-Gaussians [14] rasterises cameras and ray-traces LiDAR; XSIM [15] gives each Gaussian one opacity per sensor. SimULi [17], the closest work, supports fisheye cameras and arbitrary spinning LiDARs in one framework, observes that a single set of Gaussians trades PSNR against LiDAR accuracy as the depth-loss weight varies, and therefore splits the scene into a camera set and a LiDAR set tied by an anchoring loss.

Those are dynamic driving simulators graded against the sensors themselves. For static metric reconstruction graded against independent TLS, we ask the opposite question: is the trade-off intrinsic to sharing one set of Gaussians, or an artefact of supervising two sensors through inconsistent measurement models?

03Contribution 1 · One ray model

A fisheye pixel and a LiDAR beam are the same object

Treat every reading as what it physically is: a ray with its own origin and direction.

\begin{aligned}\text{camera pixel } u:&\quad \bo=\text{camera centre},\quad \bd = R_{w\leftarrow c}\,\pi_c^{-1}(u)\\ \text{LiDAR return } k:&\quad \bo_k=\text{origin at the return's own timestamp},\quad \bd_k=\text{beam},\quad \rho_k=\text{range}\end{aligned}

\pi_c is the calibrated fisheye model (equidistant on Oxford Spires). The whole valid image circle is used at native resolution and never resampled; there is no crop and no virtual depth camera.

Where a ray meets a Gaussian

\begin{gathered}\tau_i^*=\frac{\bd^\top\Sigma_i^{-1}(\bmu_i-\bo)}{\bd^\top\Sigma_i^{-1}\bd}\\[6pt] D_i^2=(\bmu_i-\bo)^\top\Sigma_i^{-1}(\bmu_i-\bo)-\tau_i^{*2}\,\bd^\top\Sigma_i^{-1}\bd,\qquad a_i=\alpha_i\,e^{-\frac12 D_i^2}\end{gathered}

Read it like this

Stretch space with \Sigma_i^{-1/2} until the Gaussian becomes a round ball. Straight lines stay straight, so the ray is still a ray. In that stretched space \tau_i^* is just the foot of the perpendicular from the ball’s centre to the ray, and D_i is the length of that perpendicular. The ray passes closest to the Gaussian at \tau_i^*, and the Gaussian contributes opacity a_i according to how close.

No projection, no Jacobian, no affine approximation. This is the peak response of 3DGRT [7], and 3DGEER [6] shows that it equals the closed-form integral of the 3D Gaussian along the ray.

What to watch

  1. Slide the ray across the Gaussian. The proxy’s centre depth never moves; \tau^* follows the Gaussian’s surface.
  2. Drag the morph to “whitened”. The ellipse becomes a circle, and \tau^* becomes an ordinary perpendicular foot.
  3. Rotate the Gaussian so it lies obliquely to the ray. That is where centre depth and range disagree most.

One termination depth for both sensors

T_i=\prod_{j\lt i}(1-a_j),\qquad k(\mathbf r):\;T_{k+1}\le\tfrac12\lt T_k,\qquad \tau_{\mathrm{term}}(\mathbf r)=\tau^*_{k(\mathbf r)}

Sort the contributions by \tau^*, follow the transmittance down the ray, and stop at the first Gaussian after which at most half of the light is left. Its peak is the termination depth. A ray whose final transmittance stays above one half is a no-hit. Both sensors use this one number.

Credit. This is the median depth of 2DGS [18] and GS-LiDAR [19], also used by SplatAD at inference [13] and formalised by Geometry-Grounded GS [20]; RaDe-GS and GOF likewise replace centre depth with the ray–Gaussian intersection [33] [34]. We do not claim the definition. What changes is its role: the camera’s depth, the LiDAR’s predicted range, the first surface used for evaluation and the evidence of §04 become the same number.

A gradient that pushes along the beam

\frac{\partial\tau_k^*}{\partial\bmu_k}=\frac{\Sigma_k^{-1}\bd}{\bd^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}\bd}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \bd^\top\frac{\partial\tau_k^*}{\partial\bmu_k}=1

The identity on the right is the intuition: slide the terminating Gaussian one centimetre along the beam, and its termination depth slides one centimetre. A range residual therefore moves geometry along the line of sight, which repairs mismatch iii. For a flat Gaussian, \Sigma^{-1}\bd points mostly along its normal, so the push goes through the surface rather than across it.

What the LiDAR is asked to explain

\mathcal L_{\mathrm{lidar}}=\underbrace{\psi_\delta\big(\tau_{\mathrm{term}}-\rho\big)}_{\text{stop where it returned}}\;+\;\lambda_{\mathrm{fs}}\underbrace{\big(1-T(\rho-\kappa\sigma_\rho)\big)}_{\text{arrive almost unattenuated}}

\psi_\delta is a Huber penalty and T(\tau) the transmittance accumulated up to range \tau. The first term asks the beam to terminate where it returned. The second asks that the space the laser has just crossed is empty up to a noise margin \kappa\sigma_\rho, the line-of-sight idea of Urban Radiance Fields [22]. A beam without a return may optionally be asked to stay empty, 1-T(\tau_{\max}). The camera keeps \mathcal L_{\mathrm{rgb}}=(1-\lambda)\lVert C-\hat C\rVert_1+\lambda\,\text{D-SSIM} with a per-exposure affine colour correction, and has no depth loss of its own: the LiDAR already shapes the surface that the camera rays composite.

The claim, stated so that it can fail

One set of Gaussians with one measurement model moves outside the photometric–metric trade-off curve that loss weighting traces, rather than to another point on it. If the C1 point lands on the curve, SimULi’s factorisation is the better representation, and C1 is reported as an implementation choice.

The threshold one half is the photometric median, while a LiDAR return is closer to “the first sufficiently reflective surface”. Sharing one threshold is therefore itself tested, at 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7.

04Contribution 2 · Evidence-gated optimisation

Where the LiDAR has measured, geometry is a constraint, not a loss

The usual way to give a radiance field geometry is to add a loss: distortion [24], normal and planarity terms [18] [23], depth and line-of-sight terms [21] [22]. Every one comes with a weight, and a weight picks one point on a trade-off:

\min_\theta\;\; f_{\mathrm{rgb}}(\theta)\;+\;\lambda\,f_{\mathrm{geo}}(\theta)

For a fixed \lambda, the minimiser is where a straight line of slope -\lambda first touches the set of achievable (f_{\mathrm{geo}},f_{\mathrm{rgb}}) pairs. Sweeping \lambda rolls that line around the set, so it only ever touches the convex hull; any stretch of the front that bulges outward is unreachable at every weight [25]. A constraint, \min f_{\mathrm{rgb}}\ \text{s.t.}\ f_{\mathrm{geo}}\le\varepsilon, has no such blind spot. And a loss never removes a degree of freedom: the normal direction of a measured surface stays available to the photometric gradient, which can still buy appearance by moving the surface.

Evidence, counted along the same rays

Because both sensors now reach the same Gaussians through \tau_{\mathrm{term}}, the LiDAR pass can attribute every return. For each beam with a return \rho, Gaussian i accumulates

\begin{aligned} h_i &\mathrel{+}= 1 && \text{if } i=k(\mathbf r)\ \text{and}\ |\tau_i^*-\rho|\le\kappa\sigma_\rho\\ f_i &\mathrel{+}= a_iT_i && \text{if } \tau_i^*\lt\rho-\kappa\sigma_\rho\\ n_i &\mathrel{+}= 1 && \text{if } T_i\gt\epsilon \end{aligned}
  • Hit h_i: the Gaussian is the surface the beam returned from.
  • Violation f_i: it blocks space the beam has proved empty, weighted by how much light it took.
  • Observation n_i: the beam reached it at all.

Every 100 steps an exponential moving average of (h_i,f_i,n_i) assigns one of three states:

◆Anchoredn_i\ge n_{\min}\ \wedge\ h_i/n_i\ge\eta_hmeasured, and it is the surface
✕Contradictedf_i/n_i\ge\eta_f\ \wedge\ h_i/n_i\lt\eta_hsits where the laser saw nothing
○Unobservedn_i\lt n_{\min}the LiDAR is silent here

Anything else is treated as unobserved at a reduced learning rate.

The gate

\Delta\bmu_i^{\mathrm{rgb}}\;\leftarrow\;\big(I-\bn_i\bn_i^{\top}\big)\,\Delta\bmu_i^{\mathrm{rgb}},\qquad s_{i,\min}\le\sigma_\rho

For an anchored Gaussian with normal \bn_i (its shortest axis), the LiDAR’s own update is left unchanged and the photometric update of its position is projected onto the tangent plane. I-\bn\bn^\top keeps every motion along the surface and deletes motion through it: appearance may still slide a Gaussian along the wall, but may no longer pull it off. Its normal scale is clamped to the sensor noise (\sigma_\rho\approx 3 cm for the Hesai QT64), its rotation is bounded per step, and it is split only within the tangent plane. A contradicted Gaussian has its opacity decayed and is pruned below a threshold; it is never cloned. An unobserved Gaussian is optimised and densified exactly as in 3DGS.

Key takeaway

No weight is tuned. The gate removes one degree of freedom from the photometric loss where, and only where, the LiDAR measured it. It is also only definable because \tau_{\mathrm{term}} is shared: without C1 the counters would mix three depth statistics.

The closest work, and the prediction that separates us

EnerGS [26] already labels LiDAR evidence in three states and already decouples Gaussian positions from the photometric loss, so neither idea is new here. The difference is granularity and where the evidence comes from:

Falsifiable prediction

In regions the cameras see but the LiDAR never returns from, C2’s completeness against TLS should exceed EnerGS’s. If it does not, C2 has no measurable difference from its closest neighbour and is reported as an engineering choice on top of C1.

05Contribution 3 · Implementation

Faithful, at no extra cost

Exact ray methods are usually assumed to be slow: 3DGRT [7] builds a BVH, and 3DGEER [6] showed that exact responses can approach rasterisation speed without one. Our trainer already carries most of the rasterisation lineage’s accelerations: GPU-resident structure-of-arrays storage, tile binning with warp-cooperative duplication, radix sorting, a selective Adam that updates only visible Gaussians [29], fused SSIM and a warp-shuffle backward pass. The claim of C3 is deliberately narrow: C1 and C2 do not need to remove any of them.

Why the exact response is cheap

Everything in \tau^* and D^2 that does not depend on the pixel is computed once per Gaussian and sensor origin, next to the 3D covariance that preprocessing already builds:

\mathbf v_i=\Sigma_i^{-1}(\bmu_i-\bo),\qquad c_i=(\bmu_i-\bo)^\top\mathbf v_i

For each pixel–Gaussian pair only two dot products and a division remain:

b=\bd^\top\mathbf v_i,\quad q=\bd^\top\Sigma_i^{-1}\bd\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tau_i^*=\frac{b}{q},\qquad D_i^2=c_i-\frac{b^2}{q}

With \Sigma_i^{-1} symmetric and the six products d_jd_k of a pixel’s direction formed once outside the Gaussian loop, q is six fused multiply-adds. The cost that does grow is the number of pixels: a native Oxford fisheye frame carries about 2.9× the pixels of the official pinhole proxy. We expect that, not the response, to dominate any slowdown; this is a derivation, not a measurement.

Related accelerations are credited rather than claimed: Taming 3DGS [29] for budgets and sparse Adam, Faster-GS [30] for the restructured backward pass and fused optimiser, StopThePop [31] for sorting. Unlike SplatAD [13], the LiDAR reuses the camera’s binning and response instead of a second rasteriser.

Key takeaway

The cost claim is relative, because the trainer’s speed is inherited: time to reach the proxy pipeline’s final quality should be at most 1.2× that of R1 on the same RTX 5090. Above 1.2× the claim is dropped and the slowdown reported as an implementation detail.

06Validation

Four questions, each with the answer that would stop us

Validation runs on one site before anything is scaled: Oxford Spires Keble-04 [2], to our knowledge the only public dataset that combines fisheye colour cameras (three 1440 × 1080 equidistant), a synchronised mobile LiDAR (Hesai QT64) and independent survey-grade geometry (TLS at 1 cm). Every method trains on one RTX 5090 at equal wall-clock with three seeds, uses the same per-exposure appearance model, keeps poses and distortion fixed, and is scored identically: about 46 training runs in all.

One ruler for all methods. Geometry is extracted the same way for every splatting method: back-project the termination depth of all training rays, drop rays whose final transmittance stays above one half or whose range exceeds 60 m, subsample at a fixed stride, then score accuracy, completeness and F-score at 5 and 10 cm against TLS. The input-LiDAR row accumulates the training scans themselves. The blind region holds TLS points with no training return within 10 cm that at least two training cameras see. Novel views are scored in-sequence and out-of-sequence on the official splits. Intervals use a paired bootstrap grouped by timestamp; differences below one seed standard deviation are not interpreted.

Key takeaway

The order is fixed because the contributions depend on each other. C2’s evidence is defined by C1’s termination depth, and C3 carries only what C1 and C2 change. If G1 fails, nothing downstream is run.

07Where we start

A complete baseline system: the R1 of every table

The proxy pipeline that this page criticises is our own working system. Stages 0–10 run from raw dual-fisheye and LiDAR captures to a Gaussian scene in the browser. Stage 8 projects each fisheye image into three 1024 × 1024, 90° pinhole views; Stage 9 trains SH0 Gaussians with paired RGB and LiDAR updates on a native CUDA backend whose parameters and Adam state stay GPU-resident [P1] [P4]. It is fast, and it is the baseline every contribution has to beat.

One complete run, 133957, went through Stages 0–10 from raw inputs between 2 and 3 October 2026 [P3].

What these numbers do not establish

Training-view fitting is not held-out generalisation, toned metrics are not SOG viewer scores, and this run says nothing about geometry against ground truth. That is exactly why the validation plan moves to Oxford Spires, where TLS exists. Under that dataset’s original protocol, R1 reached 23.67 dB in-sequence and 21.75 dB out-of-sequence on Keble-04 in a single run at an unequal budget: a competitive starting point, not a result.

Upstream, in briefWhat happens before Gaussian training?+

Stages 0–4 handle decoding, dynamic-point filtering, synchronisation, image/pose pairing and person masks. Stages 5–7 perform feature matching, triangulation and joint visual–LiDAR geometry optimisation. Stage 8 projects each fisheye image into three 1024 × 1024, 90° pinhole views, which are different from Stage 5’s five-view feature-extraction projections. Stage 9 consumes the prepared views with the filtered LiDAR map and trajectory. [P4]

Delivery, in briefHow does a trained scene reach the browser?+

Stage 10 merges the main and environment Gaussians, writes standard little-endian Gaussian PLY, converts it to the official SOG format and emits a self-hostable SuperSplat viewer. SOG quantises attributes and does not carry the checkpoint’s per-image appearance or distortion, so browser quality needs its own evaluation. The documented transform is (x, y, z) → (−x, z, y), with the initial camera transformed consistently. [P2]

  • The experiments, pass rules and fall-backs that will decide each claim.

  • Held-out or geometric quality of the recorded run.

Known limitations

  • Calibration and timing. One shared set of Gaussians must absorb residual extrinsic and timing errors between the sensors. SimULi attributes the camera–LiDAR trade-off precisely to such errors [17]; a static handheld rig does not escape this merely by being static. Sensitivity to extrinsic and time perturbations will be reported.
  • LiDAR physics. Beam divergence, multiple returns, glass and specular surfaces violate a single termination, and one threshold may not suit both sensors; only three values are tested.
  • Ordering. Responses are exact per Gaussian, but the order along a ray follows a per-Gaussian sort key.
  • Scope. Static scenes with given poses. Dynamic objects, sensor simulation (intensity, ray drop) and pose estimation are out of scope.
  • Evidence. Validation is single-site. Generality across sites, LiDAR models and fisheye models is still to be shown.
